Executive Summary

Verified Analytical Findings

Four critical takeaways derived from direct SQL queries on the cleaned dataset. Every finding pairs a business takeaway with exact quantitative evidence, strategic interpretation, and documented limitations.

1

Upper Quartile Charge Concentration

Gross hospital billing is heavily concentrated in the top 25% of encounters, where charges average 6.2x higher than the lowest quartile.

SQL Evidence: Quartile 4 ($Q_4$: charges ≥ $37,820.49) accounts for 13,742 encounters and absorbs $604.6M (43.06% of total billing) with a mean charge of $43,997.56, compared to $7,075.36 in $Q_1$. The top 5% outliers ($P_{95} \ge \$47,638.27$) absorb $134.8M (9.60%).
Operational Implication

Revenue cycle teams must establish prioritized pre-bill clinical documentation review for claims exceeding $37,820 to minimize catastrophic denial velocity from commercial payers.

Dataset Limitation

The dataset provides lump-sum gross billing without itemized billing codes (ICU stay, surgery, implants); root clinical cost drivers cannot be isolated.

2

Decoupled Stay Duration & Billed Charges

Inpatient length of stay shows zero descriptive correlation with billed charges (r = 0.0003), disproving stay duration as a financial proxy.

SQL Evidence: Inpatient stays strictly span 1 to 30 days (Mean: 15.50 days, Median: 15.0 days). The average charge for a 1-day stay is $25,512.43, virtually identical to a 30-day stay ($25,488.19).
Operational Implication

A crucial data analyst capability is identifying data anomalies. In real hospitals, longer stays drive higher charges; here, pricing models must not rely on length-of-stay assumptions.

Dataset Limitation

Direct artifact of synthetic data generation where admission dates, discharge dates, and billing amounts were populated independently.

3

Balanced Multi-Payer Portfolio

Gross billing and encounter volume are split almost evenly across 5 major payers (~20% each), insulating the network from single-carrier insolvency.

SQL Evidence: Cigna: $284.3M (20.27%), Medicare: $282.9M (20.08%), Blue Cross: $280.4M (19.93%), UnitedHealthcare: $279.9M (20.04%), Aetna: $276.5M (19.69%).
Operational Implication

Maintains robust commercial contract renegotiation leverage. Managed care contracting can enforce standardized claim adjudication timelines without single-payer concentration risk.

Dataset Limitation

Represents gross chargemaster list prices, not net collected cash after contractual allowances and Medicare fee schedule adjustments.

4

Senior Cohort Inpatient Bed Occupancy

Patients aged 65 and older represent the largest single demographic volume and financial segment, requiring specialized geriatric care pathways.

SQL Evidence: Seniors 65+ account for 16,096 admissions (29.28%) and $409.3M in gross charges. Pediatric encounters (<18) account for only 116 records (0.21%), while young adults (18–35) account for 26.00%.
Operational Implication

Hospitals should allocate post-acute discharge coordination and home health liaisons primarily to geriatric wards to prevent discharge delays.

Dataset Limitation

The dataset does not specify secondary comorbidities, admission source (nursing facility vs home), or Medicare Advantage vs Traditional Medicare.

Analytical SQL Deep Dive

Production SQL Case Studies

Examining 4 representative PostgreSQL analytical queries demonstrating CTE chaining, partitioned window functions, dense calendar generation, and non-parametric percentiles.

Window Function: LAG() & GENERATE_SERIES()

Analytical Question: What is the Month-over-Month (MoM) intake trajectory, and how do truncated baseline years (2019, 2024) compare on a normalized run-rate basis?

Computes volume momentum without self-joins. Employs GENERATE_SERIES to create an unbroken calendar grid, preventing LAG(1) from skipping months in filtered subsets, and wraps divisions with NULLIF.

Source File: sql/13_time_trends.sql
SQL Features: CTE, GENERATE_SERIES, LAG(), NULLIF
Dialect: PostgreSQL 16 ANSI SQL
sql/13_time_trends.sql (Excerpt: Dense Calendar MoM Momentum) 
WITH calendar_grid AS (
    SELECT DATE_TRUNC('month', series_date)::DATE AS calendar_month
    FROM GENERATE_SERIES('2019-05-01'::DATE, '2024-05-01'::DATE, '1 month'::INTERVAL) AS series_date
),
monthly_metrics AS (
    SELECT 
        DATE_TRUNC('month', date_of_admission)::DATE AS admission_month,
        COUNT(admission_record_id)                   AS monthly_admissions,
        ROUND(SUM(billing_amount), 2)                AS monthly_billing_usd
    FROM healthcare.admissions
    GROUP BY DATE_TRUNC('month', date_of_admission)
),
dense_monthly AS (
    SELECT 
        cg.calendar_month,
        COALESCE(mm.monthly_admissions, 0)    AS monthly_admissions,
        COALESCE(mm.monthly_billing_usd, 0.0) AS monthly_billing_usd
    FROM calendar_grid cg
    LEFT JOIN monthly_metrics mm ON cg.calendar_month = mm.admission_month
)
SELECT 
    calendar_month,
    monthly_admissions,
    LAG(monthly_admissions, 1) OVER (ORDER BY calendar_month) AS prev_month_admissions,
    ROUND(((monthly_admissions - LAG(monthly_admissions, 1) OVER (ORDER BY calendar_month))::NUMERIC * 100.0 / 
           NULLIF(LAG(monthly_admissions, 1) OVER (ORDER BY calendar_month), 0)), 2) AS mom_admission_change_pct,
    monthly_billing_usd,
    LAG(monthly_billing_usd, 1) OVER (ORDER BY calendar_month) AS prev_month_billing_usd,
    ROUND(((monthly_billing_usd - LAG(monthly_billing_usd, 1) OVER (ORDER BY calendar_month)) * 100.0 / 
           NULLIF(LAG(monthly_billing_usd, 1) OVER (ORDER BY calendar_month), 0)), 2) AS mom_billing_change_pct
FROM dense_monthly
ORDER BY calendar_month;

Statistical SQL: NTILE(4) & PERCENTILE_CONT

Analytical Question: How are hospital charges dispersed across volume-balanced quartiles and upper 5% outlier thresholds?

Partitions admissions into 4 equal-volume financial cohorts (NTILE(4)) and computes exact non-parametric median and 95th percentile (P95) boundaries using explicit ::DOUBLE PRECISION casting.

Source Files: sql/16_cost_segments.sql • sql/17_high_cost.sql
SQL Features: NTILE(4), PERCENTILE_CONT(0.95), CAST
sql/16_cost_segments.sql (Excerpt: Financial Quartiles via NTILE) 
WITH ranked_billing AS (
    SELECT 
        admission_record_id,
        billing_amount,
        length_of_stay_days,
        NTILE(4) OVER (ORDER BY billing_amount) AS billing_quartile
    FROM healthcare.admissions
)
SELECT 
    CASE billing_quartile
        WHEN 1 THEN 'Q1 (Lowest)'
        WHEN 2 THEN 'Q2'
        WHEN 3 THEN 'Q3'
        WHEN 4 THEN 'Q4 (Highest)'
    END AS billing_quartile,
    COUNT(admission_record_id)            AS encounters_count,
    ROUND(MIN(billing_amount), 2)         AS min_billing_usd,
    ROUND(MAX(billing_amount), 2)         AS max_billing_usd,
    ROUND(AVG(billing_amount), 2)         AS avg_billing_usd,
    ROUND(SUM(billing_amount), 2)         AS total_billing_usd,
    ROUND(SUM(billing_amount) * 100.0 / 
          SUM(SUM(billing_amount)) OVER (), 2) AS billing_share_pct,
    ROUND(AVG(length_of_stay_days), 2)    AS avg_stay_days
FROM ranked_billing
GROUP BY billing_quartile
ORDER BY billing_quartile;

Architectural SQL: Chained CTE Benchmarking

Analytical Question: Which medical conditions deviate from overall hospital enterprise charge benchmarks after isolating accounting adjustments?

Computes enterprise averages in CTE 1, condition metrics in CTE 2 filtering out negative credit adjustments (is_negative_billing = FALSE), and calculates dollar deltas and percentage variances in the outer query.

Source File: sql/12_cte_analysis.sql
SQL Features: Chained CTEs, CROSS JOIN, Delta Math
sql/12_cte_analysis.sql (Excerpt: Enterprise Benchmark Variance) 
WITH enterprise_baseline AS (
    SELECT 
        COUNT(*)                             AS grand_total_encounters,
        ROUND(AVG(billing_amount), 2)        AS enterprise_avg_billing,
        ROUND(AVG(length_of_stay_days), 2)   AS enterprise_avg_los
    FROM healthcare.admissions
    WHERE is_negative_billing = FALSE
),
condition_performance AS (
    SELECT 
        medical_condition,
        COUNT(admission_record_id)           AS condition_encounters,
        ROUND(SUM(billing_amount), 2)        AS condition_total_billing,
        ROUND(AVG(billing_amount), 2)        AS condition_avg_billing,
        ROUND(AVG(length_of_stay_days), 2)   AS condition_avg_los
    FROM healthcare.admissions
    WHERE is_negative_billing = FALSE
    GROUP BY medical_condition
)
SELECT 
    cp.medical_condition,
    cp.condition_encounters,
    cp.condition_avg_billing,
    eb.enterprise_avg_billing,
    ROUND(cp.condition_avg_billing - eb.enterprise_avg_billing, 2) AS billing_diff_from_enterprise,
    ROUND(((cp.condition_avg_billing - eb.enterprise_avg_billing) * 100.0 / 
           NULLIF(eb.enterprise_avg_billing, 0)), 2) AS billing_variance_pct
FROM condition_performance cp
CROSS JOIN enterprise_baseline eb
ORDER BY cp.condition_avg_billing DESC;

Conditional Aggregation: FILTER (WHERE ...)

Analytical Question: What is the biological sex distribution and encounter volume concentration across clinical age brackets?

Constructs a demographic cross-tabulation matrix using PostgreSQL's modern FILTER (WHERE ...) conditional aggregate syntax, avoiding bulky self-joins or multiple queries.

Source File: sql/05_demographics.sql
SQL Features: FILTER (WHERE ...), CASE WHEN, Cohorting
sql/05_demographics.sql (Excerpt: Conditional Aggregation Matrix) 
SELECT 
    CASE 
        WHEN age < 18 THEN '< 18 (Pediatric)'
        WHEN age BETWEEN 18 AND 35 THEN '18 - 35 (Young Adult)'
        WHEN age BETWEEN 36 AND 50 THEN '36 - 50 (Adult)'
        WHEN age BETWEEN 51 AND 65 THEN '51 - 65 (Middle-Aged)'
        ELSE '65+ (Senior)'
    END AS age_cohort,
    COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE gender = 'Female') AS female_admissions,
    COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE gender = 'Male')   AS male_admissions,
    ROUND(COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE gender = 'Female')::NUMERIC * 100.0 / 
          NULLIF(COUNT(*), 0), 2) AS female_ratio_pct,
    COUNT(*) AS total_cohort_admissions,
    ROUND(AVG(billing_amount), 2) AS cohort_avg_billing_usd
FROM healthcare.admissions
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY MIN(age);

Analytical Boundaries

Dataset Limitations & Non-Claims

Sound data analysis requires clearly communicating what the data cannot answer. Recognizing structural limitations prevents flawed business decisions.

Structural Boundary

The Readmission Fallacy & Missing Patient EMPI

The dataset records patient names (`Name`), age, and gender, but lacks an Enterprise Master Patient Index (EMPI), Social Security Number, or medical record number (MRN). The dataset exhibits 40,235 distinct names across 54,966 rows. Common names (e.g. "Michael Smith") appear multiple times with conflicting blood types and facilities. Grouping encounters by name to compute 30-day readmissions creates spurious pairings. Readmission analysis is formally declared out of scope.

Financial Accounting

Gross Chargemaster Charges vs. Net Collected Revenue

The billing_amount column represents gross chargemaster list prices. In healthcare finance, chargemaster prices are marked up and rarely reflect actual cash collections. Net collected revenue is determined by contractual allowances, commercial fee schedules, copayments, and Medicare DRG reimbursement. This analysis evaluates gross charge dispersion, not hospital profitability or net revenue.

Data Generation

Synthetic Uniformity & Decoupled Clinical Attributes

Statistical tests prove the Kaggle dataset was synthetically generated: medical conditions (~16.6% each), payers (~20.0% each), and stay durations (1–30 days) are uniformly distributed. Crucially, stay duration and billing amount are decoupled ($r = 0.0003$). Findings describe the properties of this dataset and demonstrate SQL capabilities, but should not be generalized to real-world clinical economics.

Sampling Limitation

Provider & Physician Cardinality

The dataset contains 39,876 distinct hospital strings and 40,341 doctor names across 54,966 records. Over 99% of facilities appear only once or twice. Consequently, hospital encounter counts reflect mock string generation rather than real facility capacity, and cannot be used to benchmark hospital clinical quality or physician throughput.

Replication Guide

How to Reproduce This Analysis

Every script in this project is idempotent and designed to run sequentially in PostgreSQL or via the automated Python test suite.

Step 01

Clone Repository

Clone the project repository from GitHub to your local environment:

git clone https://github.com/NgKthy/prj-healthcare-data-analysis.git
cd prj-healthcare-data-analysis
Step 02

Initialize PostgreSQL

Create the schema and lossless staging table:

psql -U postgres -f sql/01_setup.sql
psql -U postgres -f sql/02_staging.sql
Step 03

Import & Clean

Load source CSV and execute deduplication ELT:

\copy healthcare.staging_admissions FROM 'data/raw/healthcare_dataset.csv' WITH (FORMAT csv, HEADER true);
psql -U postgres -f sql/04_cleaning.sql
Step 04

Run Validation Suite

Execute analytical scripts (05–19) and assertion suite:

psql -U postgres -f sql/20_validation.sql
# Or run Python test harness:
python scripts/run_all_tests.py
